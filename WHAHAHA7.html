The Chemistry of Gasoline

Gasoline is one of the most important energy sources in modern civilization. It is a carefully refined mixture of hydrocarbons derived from crude petroleum and engineered to release chemical energy through combustion reactions. The chemistry of gasoline involves hydrocarbon structures, refining techniques, molecular stability, volatility, combustion reactions, and environmental effects. This website explores the scientific principles behind gasoline production, chemical composition, energy transformation, and its role in the Philippine energy sector.

Explore Chemistry

Introduction to Gasoline

Gasoline is a volatile and highly flammable liquid fuel produced through the refining of crude oil. It contains different hydrocarbon compounds, mostly composed of carbon and hydrogen atoms. Most gasoline molecules range from C5 to C12 hydrocarbons. Because gasoline vaporizes easily, it mixes rapidly with oxygen and undergoes combustion reactions that release large amounts of energy. Gasoline is widely used because of its high energy density, rapid ignition properties, and efficient storage and transportation capabilities.

Chemical Composition

Gasoline is not a single pure substance. It is a mixture of hydrocarbons including alkanes, cycloalkanes, and aromatic hydrocarbons.

  • Pentane (C₅H₁₂)
  • Hexane (C₆H₁₄)
  • Heptane (C₇H₁₆)
  • Octane (C₈H₁₈)
  • Aromatic compounds

Properties of Gasoline

Gasoline has several important chemical and physical properties that make it an effective fuel. Its volatility allows easy vaporization, while its hydrocarbon bonds store large amounts of chemical energy.

  • Highly flammable
  • Volatile liquid fuel
  • High energy density
  • Rapid vaporization
  • Contains fuel additives

Hydrocarbons and Gasoline Molecules

Hydrocarbons are organic compounds composed exclusively of carbon and hydrogen atoms. They are the primary chemical constituents of petroleum and gasoline. Different hydrocarbon chain lengths determine volatility, ignition behavior, boiling point, and fuel performance.

What are Hydrocarbons?

According to Encyclopædia Britannica, hydrocarbons are compounds made entirely of carbon and hydrogen atoms. These compounds form the backbone of petroleum, natural gas, plastics, synthetic fibers, and transportation fuels.

  • Contain only carbon and hydrogen
  • Main components of crude oil
  • Used as fuels and industrial chemicals
  • Can form straight or branched chains

Gasoline Hydrocarbon Range

Gasoline is composed mainly of hydrocarbons ranging from Pentane (C5) to Dodecane (C12). These molecules provide a balance between volatility, energy density, and combustion stability.

  • Pentane — C₅H₁₂
  • Hexane — C₆H₁₄
  • Heptane — C₇H₁₆
  • Octane — C₈H₁₈
  • Nonane — C₉H₂₀
  • Decane — C₁₀H₂₂
  • Undecane — C₁₁H₂₄
  • Dodecane — C₁₂H₂₆

Pentane and Hexane

Pentane and hexane are highly volatile hydrocarbons that vaporize quickly. They help engines start efficiently, especially in colder conditions. Pentane boils near 36°C while hexane boils around 69°C.

Heptane and Octane

Heptane ignites very easily under pressure and defines the zero point of the octane rating scale. Isooctane resists premature ignition and defines the 100-octane standard.

Nonane to Dodecane

Heavier hydrocarbons such as nonane, decane, undecane, and dodecane provide higher energy density and sustained combustion once the engine has fully warmed up.

Gasoline Production

Gasoline is produced through petroleum refining and catalytic chemical processes. Crude oil undergoes separation and molecular transformation to create usable fuel products.

Crude Oil Extraction

Crude oil is extracted from underground reservoirs using drilling technologies and transported to petroleum refineries for chemical processing and separation.

Fractional Distillation

In a distillation tower, hydrocarbons separate according to their boiling points. Lighter fractions rise higher in the tower while heavier fractions remain lower.

Catalytic Cracking

Large hydrocarbon molecules are broken into smaller gasoline-range molecules using catalysts, heat, and pressure.

Combustion Chemistry

Combustion is the chemical reaction between hydrocarbons and oxygen that releases stored chemical energy.

2C₈H₁₈ + 25O₂ → 16CO₂ + 18H₂O + Energy

Energy Release

Breaking and reforming chemical bonds during combustion releases energy in the form of heat and light.

Complete Combustion

Complete combustion produces carbon dioxide and water vapor when enough oxygen is present.

Incomplete Combustion

Incomplete combustion may produce carbon monoxide, soot, and harmful hydrocarbons.

The Octane Rating System

The octane rating system measures gasoline's resistance to engine knock, a damaging form of premature fuel ignition inside combustion chambers.

Engine Knock

Engine knock occurs when the fuel-air mixture ignites too early under high pressure and temperature. This creates shockwaves that can damage pistons and engine components.

Octane Standards

Straight-chain heptane was assigned an octane value of 0 because it ignites very easily. Isooctane was assigned a value of 100 because of its strong resistance to premature ignition.

Fuel Ratings

An 87-octane fuel behaves similarly to a reference mixture containing 87% isooctane and 13% heptane. Higher octane fuels resist knock better in high-compression engines.

Additives and Fuel Blends

Modern gasoline contains engineered additives that improve combustion, reduce emissions, and keep engine systems clean.

Ethanol Blends

Ethanol (C₂H₅OH) is commonly blended into gasoline as E10, E15, and E85 fuel mixtures. Ethanol introduces oxygen into the fuel, promoting more complete combustion and lowering carbon monoxide emissions.

  • Reduces carbon monoxide emissions
  • Provides oxygen for cleaner combustion
  • Common blends include E10 and E15
  • Can lower harmful exhaust gases

Detergent Additives

Detergent additives prevent carbon deposits from forming on injectors, valves, and combustion chamber surfaces. These additives help maintain fuel efficiency and combustion stability.

  • Cleans fuel injectors
  • Reduces carbon buildup
  • Improves fuel spray patterns
  • Helps prevent pre-ignition

Gasoline and Energy in the Philippines

The Philippines is a net energy importer despite having domestic oil, natural gas, coal, and renewable energy resources.

Energy Overview

In 2019, petroleum and other liquids accounted for 45% of total primary energy consumption in the Philippines, followed by coal at 36%, natural gas at 7%, non-hydropower renewables at 7%, and hydroelectricity at 4%.

Petroleum Industry

The Philippines consumed approximately 474,000 barrels of petroleum per day in 2019. The country has two major refineries operated by Petron Corporation and Pilipinas Shell Petroleum Corporation.

Natural Gas and Coal

The Malampaya gas field is one of the country's major natural gas sources and provides up to 20% of Philippine electricity generation. Coal also remains a major contributor to electricity production.

Electricity Generation

The Philippines generated approximately 106 billion kilowatt-hours of electricity in 2019, with fossil fuels accounting for most of the energy production.

How Engines Combust Gasoline

Environmental Effects

Gasoline combustion affects air quality, atmospheric chemistry, and global climate systems.

Greenhouse Gases

Carbon dioxide produced during combustion traps heat in the atmosphere and contributes to global warming.

Photochemical Smog

Nitrogen oxides and hydrocarbons react in sunlight to form photochemical smog and tropospheric ozone.

Toxic Compounds

Some gasoline components such as benzene may pose health risks and environmental hazards.

Air Pollution

Nitrogen oxides and unburned hydrocarbons react with sunlight to form photochemical smog and ground-level ozone, which negatively affect respiratory health.

Benzene Toxicity

Gasoline contains aromatic compounds such as benzene, which is classified as a human carcinogen associated with leukemia and other blood-related diseases.

Groundwater Contamination

Leaks from underground gasoline storage tanks can release toxic hydrocarbons into soil and groundwater systems, requiring expensive remediation and filtration.

Retail Gasoline Economics

The retail cost of gasoline is determined by crude oil prices, refining expenses, transportation systems, marketing, and taxes.

Crude Oil Costs

Crude oil represents approximately 50–60% of retail gasoline prices. Global supply, geopolitical events, and industrial demand strongly influence petroleum prices.

Refining Costs

Refineries chemically process crude oil into gasoline while adjusting fuel volatility for different seasons and environmental regulations.

Distribution and Taxes

Transportation pipelines, trucking logistics, retail station costs, and government fuel taxes all contribute to the final consumer price.

Energy Density Comparisons

Gasoline remains one of the highest practical energy-density fuels used in transportation systems today.

Gasoline

Gasoline contains approximately:

  • 44.4 MJ/kg gravimetric energy density
  • 34.2 MJ/L volumetric energy density
  • High range capability
  • Fast refueling capability

Lithium-Ion Batteries

Lithium-ion batteries provide lower energy density compared to gasoline but offer higher drivetrain efficiency in electric vehicles.

  • 0.9 MJ/kg
  • 2.4 MJ/L
  • High efficiency
  • Zero tailpipe emissions

Hydrogen Fuel

Hydrogen possesses extremely high gravimetric energy density but requires large storage volumes because of its low density.

  • 120 MJ/kg
  • 5.6 MJ/L
  • Requires high-pressure storage
  • Low volumetric density
